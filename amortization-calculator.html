What is Amortization?

Amortization has two distinct definitions. The first refers to the systematic repayment of a loan over time through scheduled payments. The second, used in business accounting, involves spreading the cost of a long-lived or expensive asset across multiple accounting periods. Both are explained in detail below.

Paying Off a Loan Over Time

When someone takes out a mortgage, car loan, or personal loan, they typically make monthly payments to the lender. A portion of each payment covers the interest due on the loan, while the remainder reduces the principal balance. Because interest is calculated on the remaining principal, the interest portion decreases over time as the balance declines. This process is clearly shown in the amortization table above.

Credit cards are generally not amortized. They are a form of revolving debt where the outstanding balance can be carried from month to month and the repayment amount can vary. For more information, see our Credit Card Calculator or Credit Card Payoff Calculator. Other non-amortized loans include interest-only loans (with an initial period where only interest is paid) and balloon loans (which require a large principal payment at maturity).

Amortization Schedule

An amortization schedule, sometimes called an amortization table, details each periodic payment on an amortizing loan. Every calculation performed by this calculator generates both an annual and monthly schedule above. Each payment on an amortized loan includes an interest portion and a principal portion, though the split changes with each payment period. The schedule shows the specific allocation for each payment, along with cumulative interest and principal paid to date, and the remaining balance after each period.

Basic amortization schedules do not account for extra payments, but borrowers can always pay additional amounts toward their loans. Note that amortization schedules generally apply only to fixed-rate loans, not adjustable-rate mortgages, variable-rate loans, or lines of credit.

Spreading Costs

Some businesses purchase expensive items that provide value over many years. Rather than recording the full cost in a single accounting period, the cost is spread — or amortized — over the item's expected useful life. Common examples include machinery, buildings, and equipment. While technically a form of amortization, this is usually referred to as depreciation. Visit our Depreciation Calculator for more information.

In accounting, amortization more commonly refers to intangible assets such as patents or copyrights. Under Section 197 of U.S. tax law, the value of these assets can be deducted over time using a calculated amortization schedule. Commonly amortized intangible assets include:

  1. Goodwill — the quantifiable reputation of a business
  2. Going-concern value — the value of a business as an ongoing entity
  3. Workforce in place, including employee experience and training
  4. Business records, operating systems, and customer information
  5. Patents, copyrights, formulas, processes, and designs
  6. Customer relationships and customer bases
  7. Supplier relationships and vendor agreements
  8. Licenses, permits, and government-granted rights
  9. Non-compete agreements
  10. Franchises, trademarks, and trade names

Some intangible assets with indefinite useful lives — goodwill being the most common — or assets that are "self-created" may not be amortized for tax purposes.

Under IRS Section 197, certain items are not considered intangible assets for amortization purposes, including: interests in businesses or contracts, land, most computer software, intangible assets not acquired through a business acquisition, existing lease or sublease interests, existing debt interests, rights to service residential mortgages (unless acquired in a business acquisition), and certain transaction costs where no gain or loss is recognized.

Amortizing Startup Costs

In the United States, business startup costs — expenses incurred to investigate the possibility of creating or acquiring an active business — can be amortized only under specific conditions. These expenses must be costs that would normally be deductible as business expenses for an existing active business, and they must be incurred before the business begins operations. Examples include consulting fees, financial analysis of potential acquisitions, advertising costs, and employee payments made prior to the business becoming active. Under IRS guidelines, initial startup costs must be amortized rather than deducted immediately.
